AI-Assisted Innovation and the Future of Patent Inventorship

Sultan Akingbogun • 2026 Academic Research
Abstract Artificial Intelligence systems are increasingly integrated into innovative processes across software engineering, biotechnology and materials science. This paper examines how AI challenges foundational assumptions of patent law regarding human mental conception, analyzing current legislative frameworks, Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, policy options, and proposing a structured AI-assistance disclosure regime.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence systems are increasingly being integrated into innovative processes across sectors such as software engineering, biotechnology and materials science. In many cases, AI tools do not merely assist human research but generate technical solutions through autonomous pattern recognition and probabilistic modelling. This development challenges a foundational assumption of patent law: that an invention originates in human mental conception.

Under the Patents Act 1977, an inventor is defined as the "actual deviser" of the invention, and must be a natural person (Patents Act 1977). This position was reaffirmed by the UK Supreme Court in Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, which rejected the proposition that an AI system may claim inventorship. Comparable approaches have been adopted by both the European Patent Office and the United States Patent and Trademark Office (European Patent Office, 2021; United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024). Whilst these decisions preserve doctrinal stability, they leave unresolved a deeper question: how should patent law respond when AI systems materially generate the inventive concept?

This paper argues that recognising artificial intelligence systems as an inventor would destabilise the normative and proprietary structure of patent law. However, maintaining a purely human-centric framework without adjustment risks conceptual incoherence and commercial uncertainty (Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, 2023). A structured AI-assistance disclosure regime, coupled with recalibration of the inventive step standard to reflect the growing accessibility of AI tools, offers a principled and commercially viable reform. Such an approach preserves the integrity of inventorship doctrine while ensuring that patent law remains responsive to technologically mediated innovation.

Current Legal Framework

Under the Patents Act 1977, the right to apply for and obtain a patent initially vests in the "inventor" (s 7), who must be identified in the patent application (s 13) as a natural person. Inventorship is therefore not a merely formal designation; it constitutes the legal foundation upon which entitlement and proprietary rights are built. Ownership derives from inventorship, either directly or by assignment, strengthening the centralisation of human agency in the statutory scheme.

The human-centric interpretation was reinforced by the UK Supreme Court in the Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents. In this case, the applicant, Dr. Stephen Thaler, attempted to designate an AI system (DABUS) as the inventor of two inventions. The Court ruled that, as a matter of statutory interpretation, the Patents Act states that an inventor needs to be a natural person, and that an AI system cannot hold or transfer proprietary rights. Importantly, the Court did not decide whether AI systems are capable of generating inventions in a factual sense; it concluded that the existing legislative framework does not accommodate non-human inventorship.

A similar viewpoint has been adopted internationally as well (European Patent Office, 2021; United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024). Both the European Patent Office and the United States Patent and Trademark Office have rejected applications naming AI systems as inventors, reflecting a broad convergence around human innovation. The current framework remains internally coherent, but it rests on an assumption that inventive behaviour exists in human mental conception - an assumption increasingly challenged by artificial intelligence daily.

The Conceptual Strain Produced by Artificial Intelligence

As previously mentioned, the statutory framework presupposes human mental conception as the source of invention. The increasing integration of advanced AI systems into research and design processes puts pressure on the presupposition in three definite ways.

Firstly, AI challenges the traditional idea of conception. Patent doctrine associates inventorship with the formation, in the mind of a natural person, of the inventive concept. However, many contemporary machine learning systems generate outputs through iterative optimisation, pattern abstraction and probabilistic modelling that are neither explicitly programmed nor fully predictable by the human operators (Abbott, 2016). Where a developer defines a research objective and the AI system autonomously generates a technical solution that the developer did not foresee, the location of conception can become ambiguous. The human mind may frame the problem and select the final output, but the inventive contribution appears more computational instead of cognitive. This places pressure on the doctrinal language centred around mental concept, as AI-mediated innovation increases.

Secondly, artificial intelligence can complicate the application of the inventive step. Obviousness is determined through the lens of the hypothetical "person skilled in the art", who has a firm knowledge in the specific field of the respective invention, but lacks inventive capability. But as AI tools become increasingly accessible, it is uncertain whether the skilled person should be assumed to have access to such systems (World Intellectual Property Organization, 2025). If AI-assisted design becomes standard practice, maintaining a purely human benchmark risks granting protection for outputs that would be readily generated using widely available computational tools. On the contrary, prematurely incorporating advanced AI capabilities into the skilled person standard could potentially narrow patentability.

Finally, the function of particular AI models raises questions regarding transparency and disclosure. In the case that inventive features are developed from algorithmic processes, determining the degree to which applicants explain the involvement of AI for sufficiency and principles becomes more complex. The lack of clear guidance risks both strategic under-disclosure and uncertainty in validity assessments (United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024).

Policy Options

One suggested response to the challenges posed by AI-assisted innovation is to allow AI systems to be recognised as an inventor. Supporters argue inventorship should reflect the reality of the inventive process (Abbott, 2020). As AI systems become more capable of generating novel solutions with minimal human involvement, attributing inventorship solely to a human would not accurately represent the creation process. AI recognition as an inventor could help with the issue of transparency and ensure that patent law keeps pace with technological development. It can also provide greater certainty where AI plays a vital role in an inventive process.

However, this approach raises multiple concerns practically. Patent law is built around human and corporate actors who possess rights, can transfer ownership and be held accountable for their actions. AI systems are incapable of performing any of these functions, so consequently, AI inventorship recognition would require significant changes to existing legal frameworks and may create further uncertainty regarding ownership and entitlement. While this proposal has the advantage of reflecting the growth of AI's role in innovation, it also challenges the fundamental principles that the current patent system operates within.

An alternative approach is to retain the existing legal framework, allowing the continuation of inventorship requiring a natural person. Under this model, any invention involving AI would still be attributed to a human participant, like the person who developed, operated or directed the system. Supporters of this approach argue that it preserves legal certainty and avoids the need for substantial changes to established patent principles (Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, 2023). Patent law, for a long time, has linked inventorship to ownership and accountability, so maintaining the current framework allows these relationships to stay transparent and predictable. This may be especially important for businesses making significant investments in research and development, where certainty of patent rights is necessary.

Nonetheless, this approach may become increasingly difficult to justify as AI systems continue to play a greater role in the inventive process. In situations where an AI system generates an invention with limited human input, attributing inventorship solely to a human can appear as artificial. Questions may also arise regarding which individual should claim inventorship, especially in situations where multiple parties are involved in the development and usage of the AI system. Whilst maintaining the current framework provides continuity and stability, its effectiveness may be dependent on whether traditional concepts of inventorship can continue to accommodate increasingly autonomous forms of innovation.

Targeted Reform

A third approach would aim to retain the necessity of human inventorship whilst introducing targeted reforms to reflect the developing role of AI in the inventive process. Instead of recognising AI systems as individual inventors, this approach focuses on adapting current patent law systems to address the practical challenges posed by AI-assisted innovation. A proposed reform is the introduction of disclosure requirements where AI has made a significant contribution to an invention (United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024). This could improve transparency by providing patent offices with deeper insight into the role AI had in the innovation process, while also helping to ensure inventorship claims remain consistent and precise. Additionally, through retaining human inventorship, this approach avoids numerous legal difficulties associated with attributing inventorship to artificial agents.

Despite these strengths, targeted reform does not come without its challenges. In particular, it may be difficult to determine the level of AI involvement that can trigger additional disclosure requirements. As AI tools become increasingly integrated into research and development, distinguishing between routine assistance and material contribution may prove complex (Yanisky-Ravid and Liu, 2018). Additional disclosure obligations could also increase the administrative burden placed on applicants and patent offices. Nevertheless, targeted reform provides a pragmatic middle ground between preserving the status quo and fundamentally restructuring inventorship doctrine. It acknowledges the changing nature of innovation while maintaining the legal certainty and accountability that underpin the current patent system.

Conclusion

The increasing use of AI in the inventive process has exposed limitations within a patent system that was developed on the assumption that inventions originate from human mental conception. Whilst recognising AI as an inventor may appear to reflect the reality of certain forms of innovation, it raises significant challenges relating to ownership, accountability and legal certainty. Conversely, maintaining the current framework preserves stability but may become increasingly difficult to justify as AI systems play a more substantial role in generating inventive outputs.

Out of all the approaches mentioned, targeted reform appears best placed to address these concerns. By retaining human inventorship while introducing measures that improve transparency and reflect the growing influence of artificial intelligence, patent law can adapt to technological change without abandoning its existing foundations. As AI continues to evolve, the challenge for policymakers will not be whether patent law should respond, but how it can do so in a manner that balances innovation with legal certainty.

Laboratory Research Project

Probe dependent allosteric modulation of GPCRs

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are important targets for many medicines and play key roles in processes including neurotransmission and pain perception. In recent years, there has been considerable interest in allosteric modulators, which bind to sites on GPCRs that are distinct from the site recognised by the receptor's natural (endogenous) ligand. Allosteric modulators have the potential to offer advantages over conventional drugs including improved receptor selectivity and improved safety because there is a limit to their maximal effects.

An important feature of allosteric modulators is probe dependence: the effect of an allosteric modulator can vary depending on which endogenous ligand is bound to the receptor. This is particularly relevant for receptors that can be activated by more than one endogenous ligand, but it has not been extensively investigated for many newly identified allosteric modulators.

Our laboratory has contributed to the discovery and initial characterisation of allosteric modulators targeting dopamine and opioid receptors. These molecules might pave the way for improved treatments for Parkinson’s disease (dopamine receptors) or pain (opioid receptors). This project will investigate whether and how the effects of these modulators change when the receptors are activated by different endogenous agonists. Students will test a range of endogenous amines at dopamine receptors and endogenous peptides at opioid receptors, and determine how these ligands influence the activity of the allosteric modulators.

Students will use cell-based GPCR signalling assays, including bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)-based approaches, and will develop practical skills in experimental design, data analysis and interpretation. The project will provide an opportunity to apply knowledge of GPCR pharmacology and drug discovery to a current research question and to gain experience with techniques used in modern pharmacological research.

Speak

“Is it better to speak or to die?” Whilst it seems like a ridiculous question at first glance, when you really think about it, what is better? Is it better to constantly let your feelings be known to everyone, with the possibility of said feelings being thrown back in your face, or is it better to simply keep quiet until your time on Earth is complete? Would it be satisfactory to let everyone know the trauma you carry, or would it feel more pleasant to keep it between yourself and your mind until you are buried in your grave? This movie resonates deeply with me because it displays how depression is for the majority of people, and how one singular event can reshape the whole of your life. Melinda was a victim to sexual violence at a young age, leading to her calling the police, wanting a safe place. But instead her friends saw her as an “attention-seeker” and “squealer”, and they didn’t believe her story. These chain of events lead to Melissa choosing to “die” instead of speaking again, which brings me back to my question again, is it better to speak or to die?

Depression isn’t just black-and-white simple. It varies between individuals, and it can fluctuate throughout the day. You can have the greatest time of your life with your friends at a small gathering or at school, and then get home to your room where it’s just your and your mind isolated, and then it’s back again. And I don’t mean to be crude when saying this, but I truly believe the way in which you view this movie can correlate to how you view mental health. One review, and I quote, from Rotten Tomatoes says “This movie is just horrible. It’s too fast-paced, the main character literally does nothing but cower in a ball the whole time.” But when you think about what she has had to endure in the film, is it really abnormal for someone to resort to nothing but dead silence?

To say I love this movie is an understatement. I think this movie is a safe space for people who have been through similar situations Melinda has endured, and also for people who have had family members go through these situations. It is hard to often spot the signs in someone struggling with mental health, but once you recognise them, I can’t stress how important it is to be there for them. You never truly know what is going on in someone’s life, and being a space for them to vent and speak to is probably one of the best things you can do for them, and potentially even save their life.

And to all the people out there like Melinda, whilst it may seem better to not speak up due to the easiness of getting comfortable to the peace in silence, it has the potential to get worse. Find a vice. Even at first it is just the notes app in your phone, or even just speaking to yourself, just let it out, and you will feel so much better. Suicidal ideation has always been quite a hard topic for me to speak about but it resonates with me due to my understanding of how hard it can feel to live at times, but finding happiness was truly the greatest thing I could do for myself. And you are never truly a victim, but rather a survivor. The best thing you can do is continue to push through life.

So my final answer to my question? It’s better to speak.

Backrooms

Well. Where do I start. I just want to note that I did watch this only one time currently at 2am whilst fighting sleep, but I would be lying if I said I wasn’t fully attentive the whole time. But to summarise, this movie is a true piece of art. However rather than covering the whole plot like I did with “The Drama” starring Zendaya and Robert Pattinson, I want to cover the three topics that stuck out like a sore thumb to me: Addiction, specifically alcoholism, loss of autonomy and one’s self, and also grief and unsolved trauma. So let’s begin.

Addiction (Alcoholism)

Addiction is something I can always empathise with, as it is a disease one can often not control until it fully engulfs them. Clark is arguably the emotional centre of addiction with these, rarely seen sober and often seen lying about his sobriety, whether that is to mask his flaws or the fear people will not take him seriously if they discover he is intoxicated. Clark lost everything he loved due to his problems with alcohol: his wife, his dreams of becoming an architect, and ultimately his personality. Clark uses the substance in the movie for emotional avoidance, which is evident in the fact that he now needs therapy. For him the casual swig of a beer or cheap liquor postpones the feeling of grief, guilt, and fear, feelings he can’t accept until the leading moments to his ultimate demise. And the ironic thing is alcoholism isn’t presented as the root problem for Clark, but rather as a symptom of wounds he doesn’t want to confront. The drink promotes an escape from reality, but it truly reels him into a psychological labyrinth where relief and satisfaction becomes impossible. And so in this sense, the Backrooms are not just a location on the other side of the wall, they are an externalisation of addiction itself - repetitive, isolating, and practically impossible to escape, which is why he ultimately dies in the Backrooms. Clark is seen using the excuses alcohol provided him with until Mary gave him the harsh reality: that he is the problem. A lot of reviewers wanted to present Clark as the ultimate villain in this story, but I believe that with alcoholism being a disease, he is truly unaware with his own issues, even when sober, which may have created a monster, but not a villain. This can be seen especially towards the end when Mary attempts to escape the Backrooms, and when Pirate Clark is reaching out to her through the narrow walls with a final whine, rather than him trying to grasp her and eat, I believe that is the true Clark asking for help, and not wanting to be left alone and face his issues by himself. This can even be strengthened by the fact that we know the original Clark is traditionally right-handed, and the Clark that subdues Mary in the Backrooms uses his left side to do so, yet Pirate Clark reaches out in the final scene of the Backrooms with his right hand, wanting someone to truly speak to and understand why he became the internally complex character he is.

Loss of Autonomy

This topic is very rich in this movie, very frequently displayed by the characters Mary Kline and Clark. The most overlooked tragedy in Backrooms is not that Clark and Mary become trapped, but that neither of them is ever truly free to begin with. Clark gradually loses his autonomy through alcoholism, reaching a point where his addiction dictates his decisions more than his own desires. Although he believes he is making choices, his dependency quietly controls the direction of his life, making the Backrooms a physical manifestation of that loss of agency, where every path appears different but ultimately leads him back to the same place.

Mary’s loss of autonomy is quieter, but equally devastating. Shaped by her mother’s fear of anything unusual, she is raised to avoid curiosity and suppress the instinct to question the unknown, leaving her worldview defined by fear rather than personal choice. Even after escaping the Backrooms, she is denied the freedom to reclaim her life because what she witnessed makes her a threat to Async. Her capture demonstrates that physical escape does not guarantee freedom, as her agency is stripped away by an organisation determined to control the truth.

Clark and Mary therefore arrive at the same destination through entirely different circumstances. One is imprisoned by addiction from within, while the other is confined by upbringing and institutional power from without. In both cases, Backrooms argues that the greatest horror is not becoming lost in an endless maze, but slowly losing the ability to determine the course of your own life.

Grief and Unsolved Trauma

Grief and unresolved trauma sit beneath both Clark and Mary’s experiences in Backrooms, shaping the way they interpret and respond to the world long before the physical horror takes hold. For Clark, grief is internalised and numbed rather than confronted, with alcohol acting as a temporary barrier between him and emotions he is not able to process. Instead of working through loss or regret, he suppresses it, allowing it to accumulate beneath the surface where it subtly influences his behaviour and deepens his dependence on escape. The Backrooms then function as a space where that buried trauma becomes unavoidable, stripping away distractions until he is left alone with what he has been avoiding.

For Mary, trauma is inherited and conditioned through her mother’s anxieties, meaning she learns to associate the unknown with danger rather than curiosity, shaping her emotional responses before she has the language to understand them. This learned fear becomes a form of unresolved psychological inheritance, where trauma is not only experienced directly but passed down and reinforced as a survival mechanism. When she encounters the Backrooms, this conditioning intensifies her need for control and caution, but also limits her ability to fully reclaim agency afterwards, as what she has witnessed cannot be neatly separated from her sense of reality. In both cases, the film presents trauma not as a single event but as something persistent and cumulative, where unprocessed grief continues to shape perception, behaviour, and identity long after its origin has been forgotten or buried.

My Conclusion

Ultimately, Backrooms suggests that grief and unresolved trauma are not isolated experiences that can be neatly processed and left behind, but ongoing psychological environments that shape perception, behaviour, and identity in ways the characters themselves often fail to recognise. For Clark, this is reflected in the way his avoidance becomes self-perpetuating, as alcohol does not simply numb pain but gradually replaces the need to confront it at all, until even the idea of change feels threatening rather than hopeful. This is captured most clearly when he admits, “I don’t think I want to change,” a moment that reveals how deeply comfort in familiarity, however destructive, can outweigh the fear of the unknown.

Mary’s experience mirrors this in a different form, as her understanding of the world is shaped by inherited fear and conditioned caution, meaning that even her attempts to escape or assert independence are filtered through a lens of anxiety that was never fully her own. In both cases, trauma does not remain in the past but actively restructures the present, creating internal labyrinths that echo the physical Backrooms themselves. The film ultimately implies that what traps Clark and Mary is not only where they are, but what they carry within them, as unresolved grief turns into a way of seeing the world that is as confining as any physical maze.

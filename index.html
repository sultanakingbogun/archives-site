AI-Assisted Innovation and the Future of Patent Inventorship

Sultan Akingbogun • 2026 Academic Research
Abstract Artificial Intelligence systems are increasingly integrated into innovative processes across software engineering, biotechnology and materials science. This paper examines how AI challenges foundational assumptions of patent law regarding human mental conception, analyzing current legislative frameworks, Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, policy options, and proposing a structured AI-assistance disclosure regime.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence systems are increasingly being integrated into innovative processes across sectors such as software engineering, biotechnology and materials science. In many cases, AI tools do not merely assist human research but generate technical solutions through autonomous pattern recognition and probabilistic modelling. This development challenges a foundational assumption of patent law: that an invention originates in human mental conception.

Under the Patents Act 1977, an inventor is defined as the "actual deviser" of the invention, and must be a natural person (Patents Act 1977). This position was reaffirmed by the UK Supreme Court in Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, which rejected the proposition that an AI system may claim inventorship. Comparable approaches have been adopted by both the European Patent Office and the United States Patent and Trademark Office (European Patent Office, 2021; United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024). Whilst these decisions preserve doctrinal stability, they leave unresolved a deeper question: how should patent law respond when AI systems materially generate the inventive concept?

This paper argues that recognising artificial intelligence systems as an inventor would destabilise the normative and proprietary structure of patent law. However, maintaining a purely human-centric framework without adjustment risks conceptual incoherence and commercial uncertainty (Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, 2023). A structured AI-assistance disclosure regime, coupled with recalibration of the inventive step standard to reflect the growing accessibility of AI tools, offers a principled and commercially viable reform. Such an approach preserves the integrity of inventorship doctrine while ensuring that patent law remains responsive to technologically mediated innovation.

Current Legal Framework

Under the Patents Act 1977, the right to apply for and obtain a patent initially vests in the "inventor" (s 7), who must be identified in the patent application (s 13) as a natural person. Inventorship is therefore not a merely formal designation; it constitutes the legal foundation upon which entitlement and proprietary rights are built. Ownership derives from inventorship, either directly or by assignment, strengthening the centralisation of human agency in the statutory scheme.

The human-centric interpretation was reinforced by the UK Supreme Court in the Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents. In this case, the applicant, Dr. Stephen Thaler, attempted to designate an AI system (DABUS) as the inventor of two inventions. The Court ruled that, as a matter of statutory interpretation, the Patents Act states that an inventor needs to be a natural person, and that an AI system cannot hold or transfer proprietary rights. Importantly, the Court did not decide whether AI systems are capable of generating inventions in a factual sense; it concluded that the existing legislative framework does not accommodate non-human inventorship.

A similar viewpoint has been adopted internationally as well (European Patent Office, 2021; United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024). Both the European Patent Office and the United States Patent and Trademark Office have rejected applications naming AI systems as inventors, reflecting a broad convergence around human innovation. The current framework remains internally coherent, but it rests on an assumption that inventive behaviour exists in human mental conception - an assumption increasingly challenged by artificial intelligence daily.

The Conceptual Strain Produced by Artificial Intelligence

As previously mentioned, the statutory framework presupposes human mental conception as the source of invention. The increasing integration of advanced AI systems into research and design processes puts pressure on the presupposition in three definite ways.

Firstly, AI challenges the traditional idea of conception. Patent doctrine associates inventorship with the formation, in the mind of a natural person, of the inventive concept. However, many contemporary machine learning systems generate outputs through iterative optimisation, pattern abstraction and probabilistic modelling that are neither explicitly programmed nor fully predictable by the human operators (Abbott, 2016). Where a developer defines a research objective and the AI system autonomously generates a technical solution that the developer did not foresee, the location of conception can become ambiguous. The human mind may frame the problem and select the final output, but the inventive contribution appears more computational instead of cognitive. This places pressure on the doctrinal language centred around mental concept, as AI-mediated innovation increases.

Secondly, artificial intelligence can complicate the application of the inventive step. Obviousness is determined through the lens of the hypothetical "person skilled in the art", who has a firm knowledge in the specific field of the respective invention, but lacks inventive capability. But as AI tools become increasingly accessible, it is uncertain whether the skilled person should be assumed to have access to such systems (World Intellectual Property Organization, 2025). If AI-assisted design becomes standard practice, maintaining a purely human benchmark risks granting protection for outputs that would be readily generated using widely available computational tools. On the contrary, prematurely incorporating advanced AI capabilities into the skilled person standard could potentially narrow patentability.

Finally, the function of particular AI models raises questions regarding transparency and disclosure. In the case that inventive features are developed from algorithmic processes, determining the degree to which applicants explain the involvement of AI for sufficiency and principles becomes more complex. The lack of clear guidance risks both strategic under-disclosure and uncertainty in validity assessments (United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024).

Policy Options

One suggested response to the challenges posed by AI-assisted innovation is to allow AI systems to be recognised as an inventor. Supporters argue inventorship should reflect the reality of the inventive process (Abbott, 2020). As AI systems become more capable of generating novel solutions with minimal human involvement, attributing inventorship solely to a human would not accurately represent the creation process. AI recognition as an inventor could help with the issue of transparency and ensure that patent law keeps pace with technological development. It can also provide greater certainty where AI plays a vital role in an inventive process.

However, this approach raises multiple concerns practically. Patent law is built around human and corporate actors who possess rights, can transfer ownership and be held accountable for their actions. AI systems are incapable of performing any of these functions, so consequently, AI inventorship recognition would require significant changes to existing legal frameworks and may create further uncertainty regarding ownership and entitlement. While this proposal has the advantage of reflecting the growth of AI's role in innovation, it also challenges the fundamental principles that the current patent system operates within.

An alternative approach is to retain the existing legal framework, allowing the continuation of inventorship requiring a natural person. Under this model, any invention involving AI would still be attributed to a human participant, like the person who developed, operated or directed the system. Supporters of this approach argue that it preserves legal certainty and avoids the need for substantial changes to established patent principles (Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, 2023). Patent law, for a long time, has linked inventorship to ownership and accountability, so maintaining the current framework allows these relationships to stay transparent and predictable. This may be especially important for businesses making significant investments in research and development, where certainty of patent rights is necessary.

Nonetheless, this approach may become increasingly difficult to justify as AI systems continue to play a greater role in the inventive process. In situations where an AI system generates an invention with limited human input, attributing inventorship solely to a human can appear as artificial. Questions may also arise regarding which individual should claim inventorship, especially in situations where multiple parties are involved in the development and usage of the AI system. Whilst maintaining the current framework provides continuity and stability, its effectiveness may be dependent on whether traditional concepts of inventorship can continue to accommodate increasingly autonomous forms of innovation.

Targeted Reform

A third approach would aim to retain the necessity of human inventorship whilst introducing targeted reforms to reflect the developing role of AI in the inventive process. Instead of recognising AI systems as individual inventors, this approach focuses on adapting current patent law systems to address the practical challenges posed by AI-assisted innovation. A proposed reform is the introduction of disclosure requirements where AI has made a significant contribution to an invention (United States Patent and Trademark Office, 2024). This could improve transparency by providing patent offices with deeper insight into the role AI had in the innovation process, while also helping to ensure inventorship claims remain consistent and precise. Additionally, through retaining human inventorship, this approach avoids numerous legal difficulties associated with attributing inventorship to artificial agents.

Despite these strengths, targeted reform does not come without its challenges. In particular, it may be difficult to determine the level of AI involvement that can trigger additional disclosure requirements. As AI tools become increasingly integrated into research and development, distinguishing between routine assistance and material contribution may prove complex (Yanisky-Ravid and Liu, 2018). Additional disclosure obligations could also increase the administrative burden placed on applicants and patent offices. Nevertheless, targeted reform provides a pragmatic middle ground between preserving the status quo and fundamentally restructuring inventorship doctrine. It acknowledges the changing nature of innovation while maintaining the legal certainty and accountability that underpin the current patent system.

Conclusion

The increasing use of AI in the inventive process has exposed limitations within a patent system that was developed on the assumption that inventions originate from human mental conception. Whilst recognising AI as an inventor may appear to reflect the reality of certain forms of innovation, it raises significant challenges relating to ownership, accountability and legal certainty. Conversely, maintaining the current framework preserves stability but may become increasingly difficult to justify as AI systems play a more substantial role in generating inventive outputs.

Out of all the approaches mentioned, targeted reform appears best placed to address these concerns. By retaining human inventorship while introducing measures that improve transparency and reflect the growing influence of artificial intelligence, patent law can adapt to technological change without abandoning its existing foundations. As AI continues to evolve, the challenge for policymakers will not be whether patent law should respond, but how it can do so in a manner that balances innovation with legal certainty.

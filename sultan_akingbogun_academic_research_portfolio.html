AI-Assisted Innovation and the Future of Patent Inventorship

Sultan Akingbogun • School of Life Sciences, University of Nottingham • Specialization: IP & Pharmacology
#PatentLaw #ArtificialIntelligence #DrugDiscovery #Inventorship

Abstract

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in scientific discovery—ranging from molecular property prediction to autonomous de novo drug design—has fundamentally challenged established global patent frameworks. Modern patent legislation globally relies on the legal fiction of a human inventor who conceives an inventive step. This paper analyzes landmark jurisprudence surrounding AI inventorship, evaluates the economic implications for pharmaceutical R&D, and proposes a modernized statutory framework featuring an 'AI-Assisted Invention' standard.

1. Introduction & Background

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has progressed from a complementary computational tool to a primary driver of hypothesis generation and molecular synthesis in pharmaceutical research. Algorithms utilizing deep generative architectures, transformer models, and reinforced neural networks now design novel chemical entities with high binding affinities in silico before human laboratory validation [1].

However, international patent statutes, such as the UK Patents Act 1977 and Title 35 of the United States Code (35 U.S.C.), explicitly restrict inventorship to natural human persons. This statutory constraint creates an emerging legal vacuum: as AI systems become capable of generating inventive outputs with minimal human intervention, the patent eligibility of key pharmaceutical breakthroughs comes under severe threat.

2. The Legal Standard of Human Conception

Central to patent doctrine across major jurisdictions is the concept of conception—defined in US jurisprudence as the "complete performance of the mental part of the inventive act." [2] In the United Kingdom, Section 7(3) of the Patents Act 1977 defines an inventor as the "actual deviser of the invention."

When an AI platform identifies a therapeutic candidate or optimizes a crystal structure, the human researcher may merely provide training datasets or general prompts. Under existing doctrine, if no single natural person contributed to the legal notion of conception, the invention risks being rendered unpatentable and placed into the public domain immediately upon disclosure.

3. Landmark Jurisprudence: The DABUS Litigation

The international legal debate crystallized through the global patent applications filed for inventions generated by DABUS (Device for the Autonomous Bootstrapping of Unified Sentience), created by Dr. Stephen Thaler [3]. High courts in the UK, United States, Europe, and Australia were called to address whether non-human entities can be designated as inventors.

Key Judicial Holdings:
  • UK Supreme Court (2023): Unanimously dismissed the appeal, holding that an inventor must be a natural person under the Patents Act 1977.
  • US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (2022): Confirmed that 35 U.S.C. § 101 unambiguously requires human individuals as inventors.
  • EPO Board of Appeal: Reaffirmed that under the European Patent Convention (EPC), named inventors must have human legal capacity.

4. Strategic Implications for Pharmaceutical R&D

The pharmaceutical sector relies heavily on patent protection to recoup the enormous capital investments required for pre-clinical optimization and clinical trials. If AI-generated molecules are excluded from patent protection due to formal inventorship deficiencies, life sciences enterprises may pivot toward trade secrets, stifling open scientific discourse and delaying life-saving therapies [4].

5. Proposed Legislative Reforms

To reconcile technological advancement with patent policy, this paper proposes a statutory doctrine of "Significant Human Direction" (SHD). Under this framework:

  1. Inventorship is assigned to the primary human researcher who formulates the target criteria, curate training parameters, or validates functional outcomes.
  2. Patent offices require mandatory disclosure of AI system usage as a procedural requirement rather than a bar to patentability.
  3. Creation of an auxiliary 'AI-Derived Patent Category' featuring shortened exclusivity terms for fully autonomous discoveries.
